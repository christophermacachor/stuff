XVII. The Pharmaceutical Industry: Structural Corruption and the Capture of Medicine

Claim: The pharmaceutical industry engages in systemic bribery, kickbacks, and structural influence totaling tens of billions of dollars. Status: Documented.

Global Bribery and U.S. Settlements

An OECD and academic review reported that major pharmaceutical companies—including Pfizer, Johnson & Johnson, and Novartis—engaged in systemic bribery across multiple countries using consulting fees, sham research, and shell companies, resulting in over $1.1 billion in global sanctions.

Between 1991 and 2021, 234 pharmaceutical companies paid more than $62 billion in civil and criminal settlements in the United States for violations of the False Claims Act, the Anti-Kickback Statute, and illegal off-label marketing. Notable settlements include Johnson & Johnson's $2.2 billion settlement in 2013 and Pfizer's $2.3 billion settlement in 2009—the largest health care fraud settlement in U.S. history at the time.

Kickbacks to Practitioners

Rather than bribing federal regulators directly, pharmaceutical companies frequently funnel kickbacks to medical practitioners to induce overprescribing. The Insys Therapeutics racketeering case is the documented example: the founder and four executives were convicted of racketeering conspiracy involving the fentanyl spray Subsys.

The FDA: Structural Conflict of Interest

The FDA is heavily funded by industry user fees—making up roughly 66% of its human drug review budget—which critics and transparency organizations argue creates a structural conflict of interest.

The revolving door is documented and quantified. Nine of the last ten FDA Commissioners went on to work for the pharmaceutical industry or serve on drug company boards. A Science investigation of 16 FDA medical examiners found that 11 took post-government jobs with the companies they had regulated. STAT News reported in December 2025 that after several senior career officials were removed by HHS Secretary Kennedy, they "immediately popped up in industry"—at Eli Lilly, Pfizer, Merck, Roche, Novartis, and United Therapeutics.

High-level personnel controversies have also hit the agency. FDA drug evaluation director George Tidmarsh resigned on November 2, 2025, after being placed on administrative leave amid a lawsuit alleging he attempted to "extort and solicit a bribe" in connection with the Commissioner's National Priority Review program.

Institutional Corruption and Ghost Management

A 2026 paper in the Journal of Pharmaceutical Health Services Research argues that corruption in pharma is not "anecdotal missteps of certain rogue corporations" but a "product of a market structure that drives the imperative for this sector to induce profitable prescribing habits by expanding its control across the medical knowledge landscape." The authors use the concept of "ghost management"—systematic strategies to shape science, media, culture, and public narratives, and to influence experts and policymakers. The market itself is "corrupted institutionally."

Capture of Medical Education

A 2026 PLOS ONE study of the top 30 U.S. medical schools found that no school received an "A" on conflict-of-interest policies. Faculty at every school accepted industry payments, including 20 deans (16.7%) and 52 clerkship directors (19.3%). A 2005 study in Academic Psychiatry described the pharmaceutical industry's extensive role in teaching psychopharmacology—industry-sponsored lectures, drug lunches, gifts—as replacing "medical education with industry promotion in the guise of scholarship."

Disease Awareness as Marketing

Testimony from the founder of RxBalance states directly: "Inside the pharmaceutical industry, however, they are understood as part of the broader marketing strategy surrounding a disease and its treatment." Disease awareness programs are evaluated for whether they "increase awareness of a condition, encourage treatment, and ultimately influence prescribing and product sales."
